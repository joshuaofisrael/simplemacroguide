How to use the interactive graphs

The interactive graphs on this site are classroom sketches with numbers you can shift. Move one curve at a time, read the new equilibrium, and check the axes so you know whether you are looking at a single market (price and quantity), aggregate demand and aggregate supply (price level and real GDP), the money market (nominal interest rate and quantity of money), the loanable funds market (real interest rate and quantity of loanable funds), or the Phillips curve (inflation and unemployment). A change in the variable on the vertical axis slides you along a curve, and a new curve needs some other cause. Practice those moves on the supply and demand calculator, the aggregate demand and aggregate supply explainer, the money market and loanable funds explainer, and the Phillips curve explainer. The figures are for study only and are not a forecast or investment advice.

Unit 1. Basic Economic Concepts

About 5 to 10 percent of the multiple choice section.

Scarcity means a choice to have more of one thing is a choice to have less of something else, and opportunity cost is what you give up. The production possibilities curve shows the most you can make of two goods when resources are fully used, and a point inside the curve is idle resources. Demand slopes down, supply slopes up, and they meet at one price and one quantity. Comparative advantage says two producers can both gain from trade when each has the lower opportunity cost in a different good.

Unit 2. Economic Indicators and the Business Cycle

About 12 to 17 percent of the multiple choice section.

This unit is how you measure the economy before you model it. Gross domestic product adds up final goods and services produced in a country, a price index such as the CPI tracks what a fixed basket costs over time, and the inflation rate is the percent change in that index. The unemployment rate is the share of the labour force that is out of work and looking. Real GDP holds prices constant so you can see whether output itself grew, and the business cycle is the path of real GDP through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough.

Unit 3. National Income and Price Determination

About 17 to 27 percent of the multiple choice section.

This unit is the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model, which sets the price level and real GDP together. Aggregate demand slopes down, short run aggregate supply slopes up while some wages are sticky, and long run aggregate supply is vertical at potential output. A change in government spending or taxes shifts aggregate demand by more than the first round of spending, which is the fiscal multiplier. The output gap is the distance between short run real GDP and potential.

Unit 4. Financial Sector

About 18 to 23 percent of the multiple choice section.

If you want an AP Macro unit 4 review, start with the two interest rates and the banking arithmetic. The money market puts a vertical money supply against downward sloping money demand and sets the nominal interest rate. The loanable funds market puts saving against borrowing and sets the real interest rate. Banks create deposits when they lend, the simple money multiplier is one divided by the required reserve ratio when banks lend out everything above that requirement, and an open market purchase increases the money supply and lowers the nominal rate.

Unit 5. Long-Run Consequences of Stabilization Policies

About 20 to 30 percent of the multiple choice section.

A policy that closes a gap in the short run does not always look the same once wages and expectations catch up. The short run Phillips curve slopes down, so lower unemployment comes with higher inflation, and the long run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. Faster growth of the money supply raises the price level over time. A government deficit can raise the real interest rate and crowd out private borrowing, while economic growth is a rightward shift of long run aggregate supply, driven by more resources, more capital, and better technology.

Unit 6. Open Economy: International Trade and Finance

About 10 to 13 percent of the multiple choice section.

An open economy trades goods across borders and lends and borrows across borders. The foreign exchange market sets the price of one currency in terms of another, with a demand curve and a supply curve for that currency. A higher real interest rate at home attracts foreign saving, which raises demand for the domestic currency and appreciates it. Appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, so net exports tend to fall and aggregate demand can shift left, and the balance of payments records the current account and the financial account, which move together.
