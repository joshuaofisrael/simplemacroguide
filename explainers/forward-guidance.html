What is forward guidance? How central bank promises move interest rates

Explainer · Monetary and fiscal · Educational only · Updated 17 Nov 2026

Forward guidance is a central bank communicating the likely future path of its own policy, using words rather than an immediate rate change. The Federal Reserve's own FAQ on the subject puts it plainly: forward guidance is a tool central banks use to tell the public about the likely future course of monetary policy, and households and businesses can use that information when deciding how much to spend, borrow or invest, so forward guidance about future policy can move financial and economic conditions today, before any rate change actually happens. This page explains why that works, the three main types of guidance central banks have actually used, dated examples from the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England exactly as those institutions state them, the limits of the tool, and a labelled hypothetical example. It is a natural follow on to the inflation targeting explainer on this site. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax or legal advice, and nothing here forecasts future Federal Reserve, Bank of England or ECB decisions.

Why words can move long term rates today

A central bank directly sets a short term policy rate, such as the federal funds rate or Bank Rate. But many of the borrowing costs that matter for households and firms, mortgage rates, corporate bond yields, multi year loans, sit further out on the yield curve. One classroom explanation for how a short rate connects to those longer rates is the expectations hypothesis: a longer term yield is approximately the average of the short term rates that markets expect to prevail over that same horizon, plus a term premium. If that is roughly right, a longer term rate does not just reflect today's policy rate, it reflects the whole expected future path of that rate. Forward guidance works on exactly that channel. By telling markets more about the likely future path, a central bank can shift what the public expects future short rates to be, which shifts longer term yields today even though the policy rate itself has not moved. See the yield curve explainer and the yield curve spread calculator for more on how that curve is built and read.

Three types of forward guidance

Central banks have not all communicated about future policy in the same way. The Bank of England's August 2013 paper, "Monetary policy trade offs and forward guidance", published alongside that month's Inflation Report, sets out three broad approaches used around the world, citing a 2013 speech by then Governor Mark Carney as the source of the grouping.

  • Open ended, or qualitative, guidance. This gives a qualitative sense of how long a rate is likely to stay where it is, without naming a date or a number. The Federal Open Market Committee's postmeeting statement of December 16, 2008, the day it cut its federal funds rate target to a range of 0 to 0.25 percent, said that weak economic conditions were "likely to warrant exceptionally low levels of the federal funds rate for some time", according to the Federal Reserve's own timeline of this guidance. The FOMC changed that wording on March 18, 2009 to describe the same exceptionally low rate as likely to persist for an extended period, still without naming a date. The European Central Bank used the same open ended style for its first ever forward guidance: the ECB's own explainer on forward guidance says the Governing Council said in July 2013 that it expected interest rates to remain low for an extended period of time.
  • Calendar, or time contingent, guidance. This names an actual date. The Fed's timeline records that on August 9, 2011 the FOMC said it would likely keep the federal funds rate exceptionally low at least through the middle of 2013, then pushed that date out to late 2014 on January 25, 2012, and further to the middle of 2015 on September 13, 2012, as the recovery kept disappointing policymakers' earlier expectations.
  • Outcome, or threshold, based guidance. This ties policy to an economic condition rather than a date. On December 12, 2012 the FOMC moved to outcome based guidance, saying according to the Fed's timeline that it anticipated keeping the federal funds rate in its 0 to 0.25 percent range at least as long as the unemployment rate stayed above 6.5 percent, projected inflation one to two years ahead stayed no more than half a percentage point above the Committee's 2 percent longer run goal, and longer term inflation expectations stayed well anchored. The Bank of England did the same the following year: the MPC's August 2013 guidance set out in that forward guidance paper said the Committee did not intend to raise Bank Rate from its then level of 0.5 percent at least until the Labour Force Survey unemployment rate fell to a threshold of 7 percent, subject to three named knockouts covering inflation, inflation expectations and financial stability.

Why forward guidance matters most at the zero lower bound

Forward guidance can be used at any time, but it has mattered most when the usual lever, cutting the short term policy rate, is no longer available. The Fed's own FAQ makes this connection directly: responding to the Global Financial Crisis, the FOMC reduced its federal funds rate target to nearly zero and then used forward guidance to keep providing information about the likely future path of policy, since it could not simply cut the rate much further. That situation, where the policy rate is stuck at or near its floor, is the liquidity trap and the zero lower bound covered elsewhere on this site. Forward guidance and quantitative easing are the two tools most often paired together in that setting, because both aim to ease financial conditions without a further cut to the policy rate itself.

Limits and credibility

Forward guidance is conditional, not an unconditional promise, and both central banks whose own documents this page cites say so in different ways. The Bank of England's 2013 paper built in three named knockouts covering inflation, inflation expectations and financial stability that would end its unemployment threshold guidance if breached. The same paper also makes a broader point about calendar based guidance: because any change in underlying economic conditions would almost certainly change the period for which a rate should stay fixed, a fixed date is, in the Bank's own words, unlikely to be a completely credible policy strategy on its own. The Federal Reserve's own record bears this out in practice. Its calendar based date was restated twice in just over a year, moving from the middle of 2013 to late 2014 and then to the middle of 2015, before the FOMC dropped the date altogether in favour of outcome based language in December 2012. A reader should treat any piece of forward guidance as the central bank's best current statement of intent, conditional on the economy evolving roughly as expected, not as a fixed commitment that cannot change.

A hypothetical worked example

Example (illustrative)

The numbers here are made up for teaching. Suppose the current one year policy rate is 2 percent, and without any guidance from the central bank, markets expect that rate to rise to 4 percent next year as the economy strengthens. Using the expectations hypothesis as a rough rule, a two year yield is approximately the average of the two expected one year rates, so the two year yield would sit around (2 plus 4) divided by 2, or 3 percent. Now suppose the central bank instead issues credible open ended guidance that it intends to hold the policy rate at 2 percent for at least two years because it judges the recovery still fragile. Markets revise their expected year two rate down from 4 percent to 2 percent, so the two year yield under the same averaging rule becomes (2 plus 2) divided by 2, or 2 percent, a full percentage point lower than before. Nothing about today's one year policy rate changed. The two year yield fell purely because guidance changed what the public expects that rate to be next year. Try the yield curve spread calculator and the yield curve explainer to see this averaging idea against an actual curve.

Common exam mistakes

  • Treating forward guidance as a rate change. It is communication about a likely future path, not an action on the policy rate itself. A central bank can issue guidance while leaving its policy rate exactly where it was.
  • Treating forward guidance as an unconditional promise. As the limits section above shows, both the Fed's and the Bank of England's own guidance came with explicit conditions, and the Fed's calendar based dates moved more than once as the outlook changed.
  • Mixing up the three types. Open ended guidance names no date or number, calendar guidance names a date, and threshold guidance names an economic condition such as an unemployment rate. Confusing an open ended statement that a rate will stay low for an extended period with a dated promise is a common error.
  • Thinking guidance only matters at the zero lower bound. It is used there most often, because the usual tool of further rate cuts is unavailable, but a central bank can in principle use guidance about its future path at other times too.
  • Ignoring the expected future path when reading a long term yield. A longer term rate is not simply today's short rate. It reflects the whole path the market expects that short rate to follow, which is exactly what forward guidance tries to influence.

Frequently asked questions

What is forward guidance in plain terms?

Forward guidance is a tool central banks use to tell the public about the likely future course of monetary policy. The Federal Reserve's own FAQ on forward guidance says that individuals and businesses can use this information when making decisions about spending and investments, so forward guidance about future policy can influence financial and economic conditions today, even before any rate change takes effect.

What are the main types of forward guidance?

The Bank of England's August 2013 paper on monetary policy trade offs and forward guidance, citing a 2013 speech by Governor Mark Carney, groups forward guidance into three kinds. Open ended guidance gives a qualitative sense of how long a rate will stay low, such as saying it will stay low for an extended period. Calendar based guidance names a date, such as saying a rate is unlikely to rise before a certain time. Threshold based guidance names an economic condition, such as an unemployment rate, that would have to be reached before policy changes.

Did the Federal Reserve really use a 6.5 percent unemployment threshold?

Yes. The Federal Reserve's own timeline of forward guidance about the federal funds rate says that on December 12, 2012 the FOMC introduced threshold based guidance, stating it expected to keep the federal funds rate near zero at least as long as the unemployment rate stayed above 6.5 percent, projected inflation one to two years ahead stayed no more than half a percentage point above the Committee's 2 percent longer run goal, and longer term inflation expectations remained well anchored.

Why does forward guidance matter most when the policy rate is already near zero?

Once the policy rate is at or near the zero lower bound, the usual tool of cutting the short term rate further is no longer available, as the liquidity trap explainer on this site covers. The Federal Reserve's FAQ on forward guidance notes that the FOMC turned to forward guidance after the Global Financial Crisis specifically because it had already reduced the federal funds rate target to nearly zero, using guidance about the likely future path of policy to keep easing financial conditions without further rate cuts.

Is forward guidance a promise that rates will stay low?

No. It is a conditional statement, not a binding promise. The Bank of England's 2013 forward guidance came with three named knockouts that would end it if breached, and the Federal Reserve's own guidance language changed repeatedly between 2008 and 2015 as conditions evolved, including moving its calendar based date from the middle of 2013 to late 2014 and then to the middle of 2015 before dropping dates altogether. Central banks state guidance is conditional on how the economy actually develops.
