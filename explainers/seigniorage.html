What is seigniorage? How issuing money can profit a government

Explainer · Money · By the Simple Macro Guide editorial team · Educational only · Updated 20 Nov 2026

Seigniorage is the profit from issuing money. On a coin, it is face value minus what it cost to make and ship that coin. On a banknote, it is the income from the assets held against notes that pay no interest, after the cost of producing the notes. A third classroom measure, used by the International Monetary Fund, is simply the increase in base money. Those three are not the same number. This page puts the latest United States Mint coin ledger next to Federal Reserve remittances and Bank of England note income, each with the date on the source. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax or legal advice.

Three ledgers, one word

Students meet the word in three places, and the places do not share a spreadsheet.

  • Coins. The United States Mint sells circulating coins to Federal Reserve Banks at face value. Seigniorage is what is left after metal, manufacturing and transport. The Mint's notes to the fiscal year 2025 statements say this amount is an other financing source, not revenue. The same notes say the budget treats it as a means of financing, because the cash arises from the power to create money rather than from a tax payment by the public.
  • Notes and the central bank. Notes pay the holder no interest. The assets that back them often do. Andrew Berg and Eduardo Borensztein, in IMF Economic Issues No. 24 (drawing on IMF Working Papers 00/50 and 00/29), call that gross profit seigniorage as well, and they also measure the annual flow as the rise in base money. Base money is currency plus bank reserves.
  • The inflation tax. If prices rise, cash balances buy less. Holders of money pay that loss. The issuer gains, until people hold less real money and spend each unit faster. That faster turnover is a rise in the velocity of money. The velocity calculator solves MV = PY with numbers you type. It does not estimate an inflation tax.

Keep the ledgers apart. A profit on quarters does not tell you what the Federal Reserve remitted. A Bank of England payment on the note issue does not tell you the Mint's penny cost. And quantitative easing is a bond purchase paid for with new reserves. It is not the definition of seigniorage, even though interest on those reserves can wipe out the income from the notes. The pillar for this cluster is monetary and fiscal policy. Short definitions sit in the glossary.

What the US Mint actually earned on coins

Fiscal year 2025 ended on 30 September 2025. The figures below are from the Mint discussion and the statement note included in the Treasury inspector general audit of those financial statements, retrieved 20 November 2026. Circulating coins shipped to Federal Reserve Banks:

  • Shipments: 7,972 million coins. Face value 539.7 million dollars. Gross cost 505.5 million dollars.
  • Seigniorage: 34.2 million dollars. That is 6 cents per dollar of face value shipped, down from 99.5 million dollars and 18 cents per dollar in fiscal year 2024.
  • Unit cost: penny 3.02 cents, nickel 13.31 cents, dime 6.77 cents, quarter 14.53 cents, half dollar 27.81 cents. The penny and the nickel cost more than face value for the twentieth straight fiscal year.

The penny unit cost splits into 2.32 cents of goods, 0.67 cents of selling and administration, and 0.03 cents of distribution to Federal Reserve Banks. The report says the Secretary of the Treasury decided to suspend production of the one cent coin in that fiscal year, after judging it no longer necessary to meet the needs of the United States, with the 3.02 cent cost named as an influence. Suspending production is not the same claim as withdrawing pennies already in pockets. The statements do not say existing pennies stopped being legal tender.

A further 8.836 million dollars of seigniorage, in the statement note, comes from circulating coins sold to the public in numismatic packs. Total seigniorage in that note is 43.049 million dollars. The 34.2 million figure is the circulating piece people usually quote. Quarters carried it. Pennies and nickels took most of it back.

The Fed line that is not a weekly profit cheque

Federal Reserve notes outstanding, net of notes held by Reserve Banks, were 2,445,802 million dollars on Wednesday 18 November 2026, from table 6 of the H.4.1 release dated 19 November 2026. Currency in circulation that Wednesday was 2,497,723 million dollars. Notes pay no interest. In a textbook that fact alone looks like a large seigniorage engine. The remittance line says otherwise.

The same Wednesday, "earnings remittances due to the U.S. Treasury" stood at minus 230,820 million dollars. FRED series RESPPLLOPNWW records that Wednesday level and was updated 19 November 2026. Footnote 8 of the release is explicit. A positive amount is the estimated weekly remittance due. A negative amount is the cumulative deferred asset: net earnings the Reserve Banks still need to realize before remittances resume. It is a stock, not a payment sent that week. Reading the minus sign as "the Fed paid the Treasury 230 billion dollars this week" gets the footnote backwards.

The combined financial statements for 2025 put the same idea on a year end balance sheet. The deferred asset for remittances to the Treasury was 243,481 million dollars at 31 December 2025, up from 215,955 million at 31 December 2024. "Earnings remittances to the Treasury, net" was a negative 21,245 million dollars for 2025, after a negative 79,104 million in 2024. Interest expense was 167,381 million dollars against interest income of 155,299 million, so net interest was an expense of 12,082 million. Of the interest expense, 147,662 million was "depository institutions and others": the bill for reserves and similar liabilities that do pay interest. Notes still pay none. The interest bill on the rest of the balance sheet can be larger than the note issue can carry.

Some districts were not in the same position as the system total. On that 18 November table, Atlanta showed 185 million dollars, St. Louis 46 million and Dallas 96 million, while the other districts and the total were negative. The 2025 statements also say certain Reserve Banks remitted in weeks when their own earnings covered costs, dividends and surplus. A system deferred asset and a small positive district line can be true in the same week. For how reserves are supplied and why the policy rate sits on interest on reserve balances, see the ample reserves explainer and the federal funds rate. For what counts as cash versus deposits, see M1 and M2.

Bank of England note profit is a different cheque

In the United Kingdom the headline seigniorage number is the note issue, not the coin ledger. The Bank of England Annual Report for the year ended 28 February 2026 says net seigniorage income paid to the National Loans Fund was 3,808 million pounds, down from 4,438 million pounds the year before. The Issue Department section calls the decrease 630 million pounds and ties it to lower interest on the deposit with the Banking Department and to higher note production costs. The financial review says the 3.8 billion pounds is income and profits of 3.9 billion, less 61 million pounds of expenses and an 8 million pound buffer the Bank withheld. Notes in circulation on the combined balance sheet were 96,209 million pounds at that date.

A Bank article on 17 September 2026, "The promise to pay: what backs banknotes?", defines seigniorage as the profit the Issue Department earns by issuing banknotes: income on the assets that back the notes, after production costs, paid to HM Treasury under the Currency and Bank Notes Act 1928. It says cash flows from the Bank to HM Treasury on that account, from 2013 through the end of July 2025, totalled 19 billion pounds. The same article says that in September 2026 about 99 billion pounds of banknotes sat on the balance sheet, of which 94 billion were in circulation with the public and about 5 billion backed Scottish and Northern Irish notes. That September stock is not the February circulation figure. Quote them with their dates.

The accounting split matters for a comparison with the Fed. The Bank pays Issue Department profit across to the Treasury even while other accounts, including the Asset Purchase Facility indemnity, can move the other way. The 17 September article says those are separate balance sheets, so a reader who nets them is answering a different question from "what was note seigniorage?" The United Kingdom page lists the Bank and the Office for National Statistics as the usual starting points. The United States page lists the Fed, the Mint's parent department, and the statistical agencies.

A real penny, then an invented central bank

Use the Mint penny, not a made up coin, for the first sum. Face value is 1 cent. Unit cost is 3.02 cents. Cost above face is 2.02 cents on that single coin. Shipments were 4,765 million pennies. Face value of those shipments was 48.3 million dollars. Gross cost was 143.9 million dollars. Seigniorage is 48.3 minus 143.9, a loss of 95.6 million dollars. Divide 95.6 by 48.3 and the loss is about 1.98 dollars per dollar of penny face value. That matches the Mint's published seigniorage per dollar issued for the penny. The 2.02 cent gap on one coin and the 1.98 dollar loss per dollar of shipments are both in the report. They are close, and they are not the same ratio, because the per dollar figure uses aggregate face value and aggregate gross cost.

The quarter is the mirror image. Unit cost 14.53 cents against a 25 cent face leaves 10.47 cents of cost below face on the unit cost line. Aggregate seigniorage on quarters was a gain of 130.5 million dollars, on 310.9 million dollars of face value shipped. One coin can lose money for twenty years and the whole circulating programme can still show a small gain, if the quarters are large enough. In fiscal year 2025 they were only just large enough.

Example (invented)

These figures are invented for teaching. They are not a Mint, Fed or Bank of England result. Suppose a classroom state issues 100 dollars of new notes and spends 1 dollar printing them. Coin style seigniorage is 99 dollars. Suppose the central bank then holds bonds that pay 4 dollars of interest and, because of a large reserve stock created alongside the notes, pays 6 dollars of interest to banks. Net interest is a loss of 2 dollars. The notes still pay the holder nothing. The institution can lose money anyway. Try the velocity calculator if you want the identity that links a money stock to nominal GDP. Nothing in that tool, or in this box, says a government should issue more notes or fewer.

Common exam mistakes

  • One word, one number. Coin seigniorage, note seigniorage and the rise in base money answer different questions. Name the ledger.
  • A negative H.4.1 remittance is a giant weekly payment. The footnote says a negative number is the deferred asset stock. A positive number is the weekly amount due.
  • Quantitative easing is seigniorage. Buying bonds with new reserves is a monetary operation. The revenue definition is the coin gap or the note income. See quantitative easing and fiscal dominance for the neighbouring ideas.
  • The penny gap and the per dollar loss are identical. On the unit cost line the penny is 2.02 cents over face. On the shipment ledger the loss is 1.98 dollars per dollar of penny face value. Both are in the fiscal year 2025 report. Do not swap them.
  • A UK note profit and a US coin profit are the same concept with a different currency sign. The Bank of England figure cited here is Issue Department profit. The Mint figure is circulating coins. The Fed remittance is a third account, and lately it has been a deferred asset.

Frequently asked questions

What is seigniorage?

Seigniorage is the profit from issuing money. On coins, the United States Mint measures it as face value minus the cost of making and distributing the coin. On notes, it is the income earned on assets that back currency which pays no interest, after the cost of producing the notes. The International Monetary Fund also describes a flow measure: the increase in base money.

What did US coin seigniorage equal in fiscal year 2025?

The Mint figures for fiscal year 2025, the year ended 30 September 2025, show circulating seigniorage of 34.2 million dollars on coins shipped to Federal Reserve Banks. That is 6 cents per dollar of face value shipped. Pennies and nickels lost money. Dimes and quarters earned it. The penny unit cost was 3.02 cents and the nickel unit cost was 13.31 cents, the twentieth straight fiscal year both cost more than face value. Retrieved 20 November 2026.

Does the Federal Reserve send seigniorage to the Treasury every week?

Not in the recent data. On the H.4.1 release dated 19 November 2026, the Wednesday 18 November 2026 level of earnings remittances due to the Treasury was minus 230,820 million dollars. The release says a negative number is the cumulative deferred asset, the net earnings the Reserve Banks need before remittances resume. It is not a payment sent that week. At 31 December 2025 the combined deferred asset was 243,481 million dollars.

How large was Bank of England note seigniorage?

In the year ended 28 February 2026 the Bank paid 3,808 million pounds of net seigniorage to the National Loans Fund, down from 4,438 million pounds the year before. The annual report describes that as Issue Department profit on the note issue. A 17 September 2026 Bank article says seigniorage cash flows to HM Treasury from 2013 through the end of July 2025 totalled 19 billion pounds.

Is seigniorage the same as quantitative easing?

No. Seigniorage is revenue from issuing coins or notes. Quantitative easing is a central bank buying bonds with newly created reserves. The two can sit on the same balance sheet, and interest paid on those reserves can offset the income from notes. This site treats them on separate pages, including the quantitative easing explainer.
