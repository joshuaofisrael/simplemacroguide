A trade deficit is a capital inflow, until the discrepancy

Explainer · Open economy · By the Simple Macro Guide editorial team · Educational only · Updated 21 Nov 2026

A trade deficit is a capital inflow. If a country buys more from the rest of the world than it sells, the extra spending has to be paid for by selling assets or borrowing. In the classroom that identity is exact: net exports equal net capital outflow. In the published accounts the two sides still mirror each other, but only after a small capital account and a statistical discrepancy, and only if you read the sign the way the statisticians do. A minus on the financial account is net borrowing. It is the inflow, not a second deficit. The second quarter of 2026 is a clean place to see the gap, because the Bureau of Economic Analysis and the Office for National Statistics both published that quarter, and neither pair of headline lines is equal. This page is educational only. It is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice, and it does not say whether a deficit is something a government should close.

Two identities, not one

Keep the textbook sentence and the statistical sentence apart. They answer different questions.

  • Textbook. Net exports equal exports of goods and services minus imports of goods and services. Net capital outflow equals what residents buy in foreign assets minus what foreigners buy in domestic assets. The course sets them equal. A negative net capital outflow is a capital inflow. The balance of payments page sorts which transactions go in which account. This page is about why the totals move together.
  • Published accounts. The current account is net exports plus net primary income plus net secondary income. The capital account is a thin set of capital transfers. The financial account is cross border buying and selling of assets. The Bureau of Economic Analysis signs that last account as net lending if the number is positive and net borrowing if it is negative. A statistical discrepancy closes whatever the surveys miss.

So the mirror is real, and it is not "the two headline numbers are the same integer." A current account deficit is paid for by net borrowing from abroad. The published financial account is that borrowing, after the capital account and the discrepancy have taken their share. Students who stop at the first two lines and declare the identity broken have skipped the lines the identity actually uses.

The US quarter, line by line

Figures in this section were read on 21 November 2026. The international transactions release is dated 24 September 2026. The next one, for the third quarter, is scheduled for 18 December 2026, so these lines are still the latest international accounts. They are seasonally adjusted quarterly levels, in millions or billions of dollars, not annual rates.

  • Current account. FRED series IEABC is minus 246,023 million dollars. The source note says the series is the Bureau of Economic Analysis, and the release rounds it to a deficit of 246.0 billion dollars. That is 3.0 percent of current dollar GDP, up from 2.7 percent. The revised first quarter on the same FRED series is minus 212,597 million dollars, which the release rounds to a deficit of 212.6 billion dollars. The widening is 33.4 billion dollars, or 15.7 percent, as the release states.
  • Financial account. The release says net financial account transactions were minus 369.7 billion dollars, "reflecting net U.S. borrowing from foreign residents." Foreign assets of US residents rose by 663.3 billion dollars. Liabilities to foreign residents rose by 978.9 billion dollars. Subtract liabilities from assets and the rounded lines give minus 315.6 billion dollars. The published net is minus 369.7. The release defines that net to include financial derivatives, so the rounded headlines leave the rest of the net, 54.1 billion dollars of borrowing, in the derivatives term. This page did not open a separate derivatives series.
  • Capital account. Capital transfer receipts fell by 1.1 billion dollars to 2.3 billion. Payments fell by 0.9 billion dollars to 1.0 billion. On those rounded lines the capital account is a surplus of 1.3 billion dollars. It is small next to either headline.
  • Statistical discrepancy. FRED series IEASD is minus 124,925 million dollars, updated the same morning as the release, 24 September 2026.

The gap of 124.977 and the discrepancy of 124.925 differ by 0.052 billion dollars. That leftover sits inside the tenth of a billion the release uses when it prints 369.7, 2.3, and 1.0. The accounts are built to match. They do not match if you stop after the current account and the financial account and ignore the other two lines. A textbook that sets the sides equal before income, transfers, the capital account, and the discrepancy is using a shortcut. The shortcut is the right exam sentence. It is not the published table.

Net exports are not the current account

The same release's revision table for the first quarter splits the current account, in billions of dollars, seasonally adjusted. The goods balance was a deficit of 250.9. The services balance was a surplus of 92.1. Primary income was a deficit of 15.8. Secondary income was a deficit of 38.0. Add them as deficits and surpluses and you get the revised current account deficit of 212.6. Goods and services together were a deficit of 158.8 billion dollars (250.9 minus 92.1). Income and transfers made the current account deficit larger by 53.8 billion dollars. Anyone who calls that goods and services line "the current account" is off by that 53.8.

There is a second trap in the units. FRED series NETEXP, net exports in the GDP accounts, was a deficit of 880.263 billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026 and a deficit of 1,217.090 billion dollars in the third quarter, at a seasonally adjusted annual rate. Those observations were updated 29 October 2026. The next GDP release is 25 November 2026, which is still ahead of us as this page is written, so the third quarter annual rate is the latest net exports figure in the GDP accounts. Do not lay 880.263 next to 246.023 and call the difference a fact. One is an annual rate for goods and services in the GDP accounts. The other is a quarterly level for the whole current account. Dividing the annual rate by four is a classroom sketch, not a figure the Bureau of Economic Analysis published. The GDP accounts have also already moved on to the third quarter. The international transactions accounts have not.

The same quarter in the United Kingdom

The Office for National Statistics bulletin dated 30 September 2026, "Balance of payments, UK: April to June 2026," gives three different deficit numbers for one quarter. The underlying current account deficit, excluding precious metals, narrowed to 11.3 billion pounds, or 1.4 percent of GDP, from 14.3 billion pounds in the previous quarter. The current account deficit including trade in precious metals narrowed by 1.2 billion pounds to 19.9 billion pounds, or 2.5 percent of GDP. The trade deficit excluding precious metals narrowed to 4.2 billion pounds. The financial account recorded a net inflow of 29.7 billion pounds, after a net inflow of 14.2 billion pounds in the first quarter.

Those lines do not match, and the bulletin tells you why you should not force them to. It says a current account deficit places the UK as a net borrower, and that the UK must attract net financial inflows to finance the current and capital account deficit. It also says the headline current account figures are seasonally adjusted, while the financial account figures are not. A seasonally adjusted deficit of 19.9 billion pounds and a financial inflow of 29.7 billion pounds that is not seasonally adjusted are not two readings of one identity. The United Kingdom page is the starting point for ONS releases. The United States page is the starting point for the BEA and FRED series above. Do not subtract a UK inflow from a US deficit.

Where the currency graph comes in

An inflow is a purchase of domestic assets, and buying those assets means buying the currency. On the foreign exchange market graph, that is extra demand for the home currency, so the currency appreciates, other things equal. Appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, which pushes net exports down. That is the second loop: capital inflows and net exports move together in the accounts, and the exchange rate is one reason they keep doing so. The purchasing power parity page is about whether the rate itself looks high or low against a basket. The purchasing power parity calculator turns two basket prices into a PPP rate. It does not compute a current account.

The interest rate is the usual reason the inflow changes. A higher real rate at home draws foreign saving. In a closed economy that extra government borrowing only competes with domestic savers, which is crowding out. In an open economy the textbook adds foreign saving to the supply of loanable funds, so the real rate may rise by less than the closed drawing shows. This page does not estimate how large that shift is. The graph, and a dated Congressional Budget Office example, stay on the crowding out page. The closed economy saving market is the loanable funds explainer. Net exports are also a term in GDP, so a change that sticks shows up as a shift of aggregate demand. The pillar for the whole cluster is the international trade hub, and the unit list is the AP Macroeconomics study hub.

A made up country, then the real penny of the gap

Example (invented)

These four numbers are invented. They are not a BEA or ONS result. Suppose exports are 80 and imports are 100. Net exports are a deficit of 20. The textbook says net capital outflow is also a deficit of 20, which is a capital inflow of 20. Now add a primary income deficit of 5 and no transfers. The current account deficit is 25. The textbook that stopped at net exports is already 5 short of the current account. Add a statistical discrepancy of minus 7 and a capital account of zero, and financial account net borrowing has to be 32 for the published identity to close. Nothing in the box says the country should borrow, or lend, or move the exchange rate.

The US second quarter is the same shape, with real lines. The current account deficit is 246.0 billion dollars on the release's rounding. Financial account net borrowing is 369.7 billion dollars. The textbook shortcut would have set those equal. The discrepancy of 124.925 billion dollars, plus a capital account surplus of about 1.3 billion dollars, is why a careful reader does not. The first quarter goods and services deficit of 158.8 billion dollars against a current account deficit of 212.6 billion dollars is the other caution: even "the trade balance" is not one number until you say whether income is in it.

Common exam mistakes

  • Net exports and the current account are one number. Income and transfers sit in the current account. In the revised first quarter of 2026 they turned a goods and services deficit of 158.8 billion dollars into a current account deficit of 212.6 billion dollars.
  • The financial account minus means the identity failed. On the BEA sign, minus means net borrowing, which is the inflow that finances a current account deficit. The UK bulletin states the same fact as a "net inflow" and uses a positive wording. Read the sign note before you compare the countries.
  • An annual rate and a quarterly level are the same object. NETEXP of 880.263 billion dollars is an annual rate. IEABC of 246,023 million dollars is a quarterly level. They are also different accounts.
  • The discrepancy is a rounding error you can ignore in the data. In this quarter it is minus 124,925 million dollars. That is about half the current account deficit. It is the reason 246 and 370 are not the same headline.
  • A deficit is a verdict. The accounts record net borrowing. They do not grade it, and they are not a case for a currency, a bond, or a fund. For the rate itself, use the foreign exchange page and the PPP calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Why do the current account and the financial account mirror each other?

A country that spends more abroad than it earns has to finance the gap by selling assets or borrowing. That financing is the financial account. In the textbook, net exports equal net capital outflow, so the two sides match exactly. In the published accounts they match only after a small capital account and a statistical discrepancy. The minus signs point the same way: a current account deficit lines up with net borrowing, which is a capital inflow.

What was the US current account balance in the second quarter of 2026?

FRED series IEABC, which republishes the Bureau of Economic Analysis international transactions release of 24 September 2026, shows a seasonally adjusted current account balance of minus 246,023 million dollars. The news release rounds that to a deficit of 246.0 billion dollars, equal to 3.0 percent of current dollar GDP, up from 2.7 percent in the revised first quarter. Net financial account transactions were minus 369.7 billion dollars, which the release calls net borrowing from foreign residents. Retrieved 21 November 2026.

Are net exports the same as the current account?

No. Net exports are exports of goods and services minus imports of goods and services. The current account also includes primary income, such as interest and dividends, and secondary income, such as transfers. In the revised first quarter of 2026, the same BEA release put the goods and services balance at a deficit of 158.8 billion dollars and the current account at a deficit of 212.6 billion dollars. The extra gap was income and transfers.

Why is the US financial account negative if it records an inflow?

The Bureau of Economic Analysis signs the financial account as net lending (positive) or net borrowing (negative). A negative number is net borrowing from foreign residents, which is a capital inflow. It is the mirror of a current account deficit, not a second deficit. Textbook pages that call a capital inflow positive have flipped that sign.

Did the UK current account and financial account match in the same quarter?

Not as a single pair of equal numbers. The Office for National Statistics bulletin of 30 September 2026 says the UK current account deficit, including precious metals, was 19.9 billion pounds in the second quarter of 2026, or 2.5 percent of GDP. The underlying deficit, excluding precious metals, was 11.3 billion pounds, or 1.4 percent of GDP. The financial account recorded a net inflow of 29.7 billion pounds. The bulletin seasonally adjusts the current account and does not seasonally adjust the financial account, so the two lines are not on the same footing.
