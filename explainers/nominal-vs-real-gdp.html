Nominal vs real GDP: what is the difference?

Explainer · GDP · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

Nominal GDP is the value of all final goods and services produced in a period, measured at the prices of that period. Real GDP measures the same output at constant prices, so it rises only when the economy produces more, not when prices go up. If nominal GDP grows 6% while prices rise 2%, real GDP grew by roughly 4%. Economists use real GDP to judge growth and recessions, and nominal GDP when they need current money values, such as debt as a share of GDP.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

Why price changes get in the way

GDP adds up very different things, from haircuts to trucks, so it has to use prices to put them in common units. But prices change. If a country made exactly the same goods this year as last year and every price rose 5%, nominal GDP would rise 5% even though nothing more was produced. Real GDP removes that price effect.

A hypothetical worked example

Imagine an economy that makes only bread. In year 1 it makes 100 loaves at 2 each, so nominal GDP is 200. In year 2 it makes 105 loaves at 2.10 each, so nominal GDP is 220.50, up 10.25%.

To find real GDP in year 2 with year 1 as the base, value year 2 output at year 1 prices: 105 times 2 equals 210. Real GDP grew from 200 to 210, which is 5%. The rest of the nominal increase came from the price rising from 2 to 2.10. These numbers are invented for teaching.

The GDP deflator links the two

The GDP deflator is nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100. In the bread example, the year 2 deflator is 220.50 divided by 210, times 100, which is 105. Prices are 5% above the base year. Rearranged, real GDP equals nominal GDP divided by the deflator, times 100. That one formula answers most exam questions on this topic.

The deflator covers everything in GDP, including investment goods and government purchases. That makes it different from consumer price measures such as the CPI and the PCE price index, which track what households buy. The CPI vs PCE explainer covers those.

How the US measures it: chained dollars

The US Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes GDP in current dollars, which is nominal GDP, and in chained 2017 dollars, which is real GDP. BEA explains the difference in its GDP learning center. Instead of using one fixed set of base year prices forever, BEA uses a chain type index that updates price weights each period, then expresses the level in 2017 prices. Chaining avoids the bias that comes from old price weights as the mix of goods changes.

In its advance estimate for the third quarter of 2026, released 29 October 2026, BEA reported that real GDP rose at a 3.4% seasonally adjusted annual rate, while current dollar GDP rose at a 5.6% annual rate. The gap between the two growth rates is roughly the rate at which the prices of goods and services in GDP were rising. The current dollar level was about $33,010 billion at an annual rate, while real GDP in chained 2017 dollars, as published on FRED series GDPC1, was about $24,611 billion. The GDP deflator for the quarter, FRED series GDPDEF, was about 134.1, meaning prices of output were about 34% above their 2017 average.

These are advance figures. BEA revises GDP as more data arrive, and its next estimate for that quarter was scheduled for 25 November 2026.

Growth rates: a quick approximation

For small changes, real GDP growth is close to nominal GDP growth minus the growth rate of the deflator. Using the BEA Q3 2026 figures, 5.6% minus 3.4% suggests prices in GDP rose at roughly a 2.2% annual rate. The exact calculation divides rather than subtracts, but the approximation is what most exam questions expect.

When to use which

  • Real GDP: growth rates, recessions, the business cycle, potential GDP and the output gap, and Okun's law.
  • Nominal GDP: ratios to other money values, such as government debt or deficits as a share of GDP, tax revenue as a share of GDP, or the size of a sector in current dollars.

Common exam mistakes

  • Saying nominal GDP rose so the economy produced more. Higher prices alone can raise nominal GDP.
  • Dividing by the deflator without multiplying by 100, or forgetting the deflator equals 100 in the base year.
  • Mixing the GDP deflator with the CPI. They cover different baskets.
  • Assuming real GDP is always smaller than nominal GDP. Before the base year, the opposite can hold.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between nominal and real GDP?

Nominal GDP values output at the prices of the period it was produced. Real GDP values output at the prices of a fixed base year, so changes in real GDP reflect changes in quantities rather than prices.

Why is real GDP usually smaller than nominal GDP?

When prices have risen since the base year, valuing today's output at older, lower prices gives a smaller number. In years before the base year the reverse can be true.

What is the GDP deflator?

It is a price index equal to nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100. It measures the average price level of everything counted in GDP relative to the base year.

What does chained 2017 dollars mean?

The US Bureau of Economic Analysis measures real GDP with a chain type index and expresses the level in the prices of a reference year, currently 2017. That is why US real GDP is reported in chained 2017 dollars.
