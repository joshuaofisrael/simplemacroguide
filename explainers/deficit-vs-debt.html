Deficit vs debt: what is the difference?

Explainer · Fiscal policy · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

The deficit and the debt are linked but they measure different things. The deficit is a flow: how much more a government spends than it collects over a period, usually one fiscal year. The debt is a stock: the total the government owes at a point in time, built up from all the deficits it has run, minus any surpluses. A useful picture is a bathtub. The deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water already in the tub.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

The deficit is a flow

The US Treasury's national deficit explainer describes a deficit as what happens when federal spending exceeds federal revenue in a fiscal year. When revenue exceeds spending, the result is a surplus. The US federal fiscal year runs from 1 October to 30 September.

The Treasury's Monthly Treasury Statement for the end of fiscal year 2026, through 30 September 2026, reports receipts of about $5.403 trillion and outlays of about $7.396 trillion. The difference, the deficit for the year, was about $1.992 trillion. The same statement shows a fiscal year 2025 deficit of about $1.775 trillion.

The debt is a stock

The Treasury's national debt explainer describes the debt as the amount of money the federal government has borrowed to cover deficits over time, together with what it owes itself. To pay for a deficit, the Treasury sells securities such as bills, notes and bonds. Each year with a deficit adds to the total; each year with a surplus can reduce it.

The Treasury publishes the total every business day in its Debt to the Penny dataset. As of 9 November 2026, total public debt outstanding was about $40.560 trillion. That was split into:

  • Debt held by the public: about $32.764 trillion, owed to buyers outside the federal government.
  • Intragovernmental holdings: about $7.796 trillion, owed by one part of the government to another, such as trust funds.

Economists often focus on debt held by the public, because that is the borrowing that competes with private borrowers for saving.

How the two fit together

In a simple world, this year's debt equals last year's debt plus this year's deficit. Real accounts are messier, because some government cash flows change borrowing without counting in the deficit, but the basic link holds. Three points follow from it:

  • A falling deficit does not mean a falling debt. If the deficit shrinks from 2 trillion to 1.5 trillion, the debt still rises by about 1.5 trillion.
  • The debt falls only when the government runs a surplus, or when other financing flows reduce what it owes.
  • Comparing either number across years works better as a share of GDP, because the economy also grows. A debt that rises more slowly than GDP is a falling share of the economy.

The UK version: net borrowing and net debt

The UK uses different names. The Office for National Statistics calls the deficit public sector net borrowing and the national debt public sector net debt. In its Public sector finances, UK: September 2026 bulletin, released 21 October 2026, the ONS reported borrowing of £24.5 billion in September 2026 alone. It provisionally estimated net debt at £3,005.0 billion at the end of September 2026, equal to 94.0% of GDP. That was the first time the provisional net debt figure passed £3 trillion, yet as a share of GDP it was 0.7 percentage points lower than a year earlier. The bulletin itself gives the reason: current price GDP grew faster than the debt.

The UK fiscal year runs from April to March, so UK and US annual figures cover different months. The ONS also notes that its recent estimates are provisional and can be revised.

A hypothetical worked example

Suppose a country starts the year with debt of 1,000 and GDP of 2,000, so debt is 50% of GDP. During the year it spends 420 and collects 380 in revenue. The deficit is 40, so debt rises to 1,040. If GDP grows to 2,100 over the same year, debt as a share of GDP falls to about 49.5%. The debt went up, but the burden relative to the economy went down. These numbers are invented for teaching.

Why the difference matters in AP Macro

Deficits show up on the loanable funds graph: government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds or reduces national saving, which can push up the real interest rate and lead to crowding out. Deficits also change automatically over the cycle, as tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises in a downturn; that is the role of automatic stabilizers. The debt matters for interest costs over time, which the ONS bulletin highlights: UK central government debt interest payable was £10.9 billion in September 2026.

Common exam mistakes

  • Using deficit and debt as synonyms. One is measured over a period, the other at a point in time.
  • Saying the debt falls when the deficit falls. It falls only with a surplus.
  • Comparing raw totals across decades without scaling by GDP.
  • Forgetting that a trade deficit is a different idea. It concerns exports and imports, not the government budget. See the balance of payments explainer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the deficit and the debt?

The deficit is the amount by which government spending exceeds revenue over a period, usually a fiscal year. The debt is the total amount the government owes at a point in time, built up from past deficits minus past surpluses.

Can the debt rise when the deficit falls?

Yes. As long as there is still a deficit, the government is still borrowing, so the debt keeps growing. A smaller deficit only means the debt grows more slowly.

What is debt held by the public?

In the US, it is federal debt held by individuals, companies, state and local governments, the Federal Reserve and foreign holders. The other part of total debt, intragovernmental holdings, is debt one part of the government owes to another, such as trust funds.

What does the UK call its deficit and debt?

The Office for National Statistics calls the deficit public sector net borrowing and the debt public sector net debt.
