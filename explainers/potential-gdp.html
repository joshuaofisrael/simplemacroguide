What is potential GDP and how is it estimated?

Explainer · AP Macroeconomics · Educational only · Updated 10 Nov 2026

Potential GDP, also called potential output, is an estimate of how much an economy can produce on a sustained basis when its workers and its capital are used at a high but sustainable rate. The San Francisco Fed describes it as the maximum amount the economy can produce on a sustained basis, and notes that output may rise above it in a boom and drop below it in a recession but tends to gravitate back towards it. Nobody can measure potential GDP directly. It has to be estimated, and the three main ways of doing that are statistical filters, cyclical indicators and production functions.

What potential GDP means

Real GDP is what the economy actually produced in a period. Potential GDP is a benchmark for what it could keep producing without overheating. On FRED, the St. Louis Fed republishes the US Congressional Budget Office series as Real Potential Gross Domestic Product (GDPPOT), described as the CBO's estimate of the output the economy would produce with a high rate of use of its capital and labour resources, adjusted to remove the effects of inflation. In the UK, the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) describes the potential level as the level of activity consistent with stable inflation in the long term.

Those two descriptions point at the same idea. Potential is not the most a country could squeeze out if every machine ran around the clock for a month. It is the level that can be sustained. Push actual output above it for long and resources get stretched, which tends to raise wage growth and inflation. Let output fall below it and workers and equipment sit idle.

The San Francisco Fed puts the core of it in one line: potential output is the product of productivity, meaning output per worker, and the number of employed workers when the economy is at full employment. So potential depends on the supply side of the economy, the number of willing and able workers and how much each can produce.

Why it matters

Potential GDP is the yardstick behind the output gap, the percentage difference between actual output and potential. A negative gap signals spare capacity. A positive gap signals that the economy is running hot. The San Francisco Fed explains why central banks care: a labour market at full employment corresponds to an economy operating at its potential, and an economy above potential creates inflationary pressure while one below it pushes inflation down.

It also matters for public finances. The OBR says it estimates the output gap partly to judge the structural budget deficit, the deficit that would be observed if the economy were operating at its sustainable level. When the economy runs below potential, part of the headline deficit is cyclical and would be expected to shrink as growth returns. And over the OBR's five year forecast, GDP growth is largely determined by the growth rate of potential output, so the estimate drives the whole medium term outlook.

Method one: statistical filters

The simplest approach treats potential as the smooth trend running through actual GDP. A filter splits the GDP series into a slow moving trend and a cyclical part that swings above and below it. The OBR calls these univariate filters, because they use the path of output alone. It gives them the least weight in its own work, because the estimate for the most recent quarters can be pulled around by the latest movements in output, which it calls the end point problem, and can be revised a lot as new data arrive.

Multivariate filters add other information. The OBR describes them as using conditioning relationships such as the link between inflation and the output gap (the Phillips curve) and the link between the output gap and unemployment (Okun's law). Because they bring more evidence to bear, the OBR typically puts more weight on them. A well known US example in the same spirit is the Laubach and Williams model, which the San Francisco Fed letter describes as a top down approach that infers potential output from data on inflation, GDP and interest rates, using a statistical method called the Kalman filter.

Method two: cyclical indicators

Another route skips the trend and asks businesses directly how stretched they are. The OBR combines firms' survey answers about capacity utilisation and recruitment difficulties into an aggregate indicator of the output gap, weighting them by factor incomes and sector shares. It also uses principal components analysis, a statistical technique that extracts the common signal from a set of survey indicators and official data such as average weekly earnings. Once you have an estimate of the gap and the published level of GDP, you can back out the level of potential.

Method three: production functions

The production function approach builds potential from the bottom up. It starts from an equation linking inputs to output, so potential output becomes a function of labour supply, the capital stock and how efficiently they are combined, which economists call total factor productivity (TFP). The San Francisco Fed describes the CBO's estimates as this kind of bottom up approach, built from a detailed analysis of trends in productivity and labour supply, updated and published typically twice a year, and deliberately smooth from year to year.

The OBR uses the same logic when it projects potential forward. It splits potential growth into population, employment (participation less unemployment), average hours worked and productivity per hour, and splits productivity again into capital deepening and TFP. It says productivity growth is the single biggest source of potential output growth and the most important source of uncertainty around medium term growth prospects.

Notice that unemployment enters through an equilibrium rate rather than the actual rate. The OBR's equilibrium unemployment rate is close to the idea in the natural rate of unemployment explainer, though the OBR notes the concepts are not necessarily the same.

A worked example with made up numbers

These numbers are hypothetical and are only here to practise the arithmetic.

Suppose a country's potential output is estimated at 1,000 billion in real terms and actual real GDP comes in at 980 billion. The output gap is (980 minus 1,000) divided by 1,000, times 100, which is minus 2 percent of potential. That is a recessionary gap: about 2 percent of sustainable output is not being produced.

Now use the simple production view. If the labour force available at full employment grows by 0.5 percent a year and output per worker grows by 1.5 percent a year, potential output grows by roughly 0.5 plus 1.5, or about 2 percent a year. Over time the potential line keeps rising, so closing a gap means actual GDP has to grow faster than potential for a while, not just grow. You can check the gap arithmetic with the output gap calculator.

Why estimates get revised, sometimes a lot

The OBR says plainly that neither potential output nor the gap between it and actual output is directly observable, and that neither can be verified even with hindsight. The San Francisco Fed letter by John C. Williams lists three challenges. GDP data are themselves estimates that get revised. Later events reveal more about the supply side, which changes the view of the past. And even with all the data in, it is hard to separate shifts in supply from other influences on output.

The letter gives a stark example. Because of a slowdown in underlying productivity and labour force trends, the CBO repeatedly lowered its estimates, and its estimate of US potential output for the second quarter of 2017 was over 12 percent lower than it had forecast in early 2007. The letter also notes that during the 1960s and 1970s many US estimates of potential proved overly optimistic, which may have contributed to policy that overheated the economy.

That is why careful users treat any single estimate with caution. The OBR says every method has its limitations and no approach avoids judgement, so it weighs the estimates from nine techniques alongside other evidence before settling on a figure.

How this fits the rest of the course

In the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model, potential GDP is where the long run aggregate supply curve sits. On a production possibilities curve, it is close to the idea of producing on the frontier with normal use of resources. Demand swings move actual output around potential through the business cycle, while growth in labour, capital and productivity shifts potential itself. Common exam mistakes

  • Treating potential GDP as a physical maximum. It is a sustainable level, so actual output can and does exceed it in a boom.
  • Confusing a fall in GDP with a negative output gap. The gap compares actual output with potential, and potential keeps growing, so slow growth can open a negative gap even without a fall.
  • Forgetting that potential is estimated. Different methods give different numbers for the same quarter, and estimates are revised.
  • Mixing up shifts and movements. A demand shock moves actual output relative to potential. A change in labour supply, capital or productivity shifts potential itself, and with it long run aggregate supply.
  • Using actual unemployment in the production view. Potential uses labour supply at full employment, linked to an equilibrium or natural rate of unemployment.

Frequently asked questions

What is potential GDP in simple terms?

Potential GDP is an estimate of how much an economy can produce on a sustained basis when its workers and capital are used at a high but sustainable rate. Output can rise above it in a boom and fall below it in a recession, but over time actual output tends to move back toward it.

Is potential GDP the maximum an economy can produce?

Not in the sense of every factory running flat out. It is the most the economy can keep producing over the long run without pushing up inflation. In a boom actual output can run above potential for a while, which is what a positive output gap means.

How is potential GDP estimated?

There are three broad families of methods. Statistical filters pull a smooth trend out of GDP data. Cyclical indicator methods combine surveys of spare capacity and recruitment difficulties. Production function methods build potential from labour supply, the capital stock and productivity. The US Congressional Budget Office uses a production function approach, and the UK Office for Budget Responsibility weighs nine techniques across all three families.

Why do potential GDP estimates get revised?

Potential cannot be observed directly. GDP data themselves are revised, later events reveal more about supply trends, and it is hard to separate supply shifts from demand swings. The San Francisco Fed noted in 2017 that the CBO estimate of US potential output for the second quarter of 2017 was over 12 percent lower than it had forecast in early 2007.
