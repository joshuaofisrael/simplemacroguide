Velocity of money: what it is and how to calculate it

Explainer · Money · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

The velocity of money is how many times, on average, a unit of money changes hands to buy final goods and services in a given period. If the economy produced $100 of final output in a year and there was $25 of money, each dollar must have been spent four times. Velocity is 4.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

The formula

The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis calculates M2 velocity on FRED as the ratio of quarterly nominal GDP to the quarterly average of the M2 money stock. In symbols:

Velocity = nominal GDP ÷ money stock, or V = (P × Y) ÷ M

Rearranged, this is the equation of exchange, MV = PY: the money stock times velocity equals the price level times real output. Written this way it is an identity, because velocity is defined to make it true. It becomes a theory only when you add an assumption, such as velocity being stable.

A hypothetical worked example

Suppose nominal GDP is $2,000 billion and the money stock is $500 billion. Velocity is 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4. Now suppose the money stock rises 10% to $550 billion. If velocity stays at 4, nominal GDP must rise to $2,200 billion. Whether that shows up as more real output or higher prices is the question the simple identity cannot answer. These numbers are invented for teaching.

The quantity theory link

Take growth rates of MV = PY and you get, approximately, money growth plus velocity growth equals inflation plus real growth. If velocity is constant and real output grows at its own pace, faster money growth ends up as higher inflation in the long run. That is the quantity theory of money taught in AP Macro. The M1 and M2 explainer covers what "money" means here.

What the US data show

FRED series M2V reported M2 velocity of 1.417 for the third quarter of 2026, against 1.420 in the second quarter and 1.413 in the third quarter of 2025, retrieved 12 November 2026. In words: over a year, each dollar of M2 supported about $1.42 of nominal GDP. FRED's notes say a falling velocity can suggest fewer transactions per dollar, with people and firms holding money rather than spending it.

Velocity is not stable in practice. It moves with interest rates, with financial innovation and with how much money people choose to hold. That instability is one reason central banks moved away from targeting money growth directly and toward setting interest rates, as covered in the Taylor rule explainer.

Common exam mistakes

  • Using real GDP in the numerator. Velocity uses nominal GDP.
  • Treating MV = PY as proof that money causes inflation. The identity holds whatever the cause; the theory needs a stable V.
  • Forgetting that velocity depends on which money measure you use. M1 velocity and M2 velocity are different numbers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the velocity of money?

It is the average number of times a unit of money is used to buy final goods and services in a period. It is calculated as nominal GDP divided by a measure of the money stock, such as M2.

How do you calculate the velocity of money?

Divide nominal GDP by the money supply. FRED computes M2 velocity as quarterly nominal GDP divided by the quarterly average of the M2 money stock.

What is the equation of exchange?

MV = PY. Money times velocity equals the price level times real output, which is nominal GDP. It is an identity, true by definition once V is defined as PY divided by M.

What did US M2 velocity read most recently?

FRED reports M2 velocity of 1.417 for the third quarter of 2026, retrieved 12 November 2026.
