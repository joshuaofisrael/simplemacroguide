The Beveridge curve: job vacancies vs unemployment, with JOLTS data

Explainer · Unemployment · By the Simple Macro Guide editorial team · Educational only · Updated 19 Nov 2026

The Beveridge curve is the downward sloping link between job vacancies and unemployment. When firms post many openings, the unemployment rate tends to be low. When openings dry up, unemployment tends to be high. US readers usually plot the Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) openings rate against the BLS unemployment rate. This page explains the classroom shape, why a point can move along the curve or jump off it, how to read the latest FRED mirrors of those series, and a labelled hypothetical example. It sits on the unemployment pillar next to the Phillips curve and Okun's law. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax or legal advice.

What the axes mean

Most textbook charts put the unemployment rate on the horizontal axis and the vacancy rate on the vertical axis. The unemployment rate is unemployed people as a share of the labour force (FRED series UNRATE). The vacancy rate is unfilled jobs as a share of employment plus openings (FRED series JTSJOR, total nonfarm). Levels matter too: JOLTS openings in thousands are FRED JTSJOL, and the unemployed count in thousands is UNEMPLOY. The ratio of openings to unemployed people is a common tightness summary. For how the headline rate is built, and for the broader U-6 measure, see U-3 vs U-6 and types of unemployment.

Why the curve slopes down

A July 29, 2022 Federal Reserve FEDS Note by Andrew Figura and Chris Waller, "What does the Beveridge curve tell us about the likelihood of a soft landing?", starts from labour market flows. In steady state, separations into unemployment balance job finding out of unemployment, so the unemployment rate equals the separation rate divided by the sum of the separation rate and the job finding rate. The note then ties the job finding rate to labour market tightness through a matching function: more vacancies relative to unemployed searchers raise the job finding rate, which lowers steady state unemployment. That is the downward slope. The note also stresses that the curve is convex: when the vacancy to unemployment ratio is already high, extra vacancies buy smaller further drops in unemployment. The FEDS Note is staff research, not an FOMC decision or forecast.

Movement along the curve versus a shift

A boom that lifts hiring demand raises vacancies and lowers unemployment: the economy slides up and left along a stable Beveridge curve. A downturn does the reverse. A shift is different. If matching between workers and jobs worsens, or if reallocation rises, the same vacancy rate can sit next to a higher unemployment rate, which is an outward shift. The Figura and Waller FEDS Note lists lower matching efficiency and higher reallocation as forces that push the curve out. Better matching pulls it in. Separations also matter: the note's flow identity shows that a jump in layoffs can raise unemployment even before vacancies fully adjust. Related classroom ideas on this site are the natural rate of unemployment, which can move when structural matching changes, and labour force participation, because people entering or leaving the labour force change who counts as unemployed.

  • Job openings level (JTSJOL): 7,523 thousand in September 2026, seasonally adjusted, same JOLTS release and FRED update date.
  • Unemployment rate (UNRATE): 4.2 percent in September 2026 and 4.1 percent in October 2026, seasonally adjusted, from the BLS Current Population Survey. FRED shows UNRATE updated on 6 November 2026.
  • Unemployment level (UNEMPLOY): 7,109 thousand in September 2026 and 7,068 thousand in October 2026, seasonally adjusted.

Pairing the September openings and unemployment levels gives about 7,523 ÷ 7,109 ≈ 1.06 openings per unemployed person that month. That ratio is a tightness snapshot, not a forecast. Always recheck FRED or the BLS release before quoting a later month, because JOLTS and the Employment Situation revise and they are not published on the same day.

A hypothetical worked example

Example (illustrative)

These numbers are invented for teaching. They are not FRED or BLS data. Suppose a labour force of 100 million people includes 5 million unemployed, so the unemployment rate is 5 percent. Firms post 4 million vacancies. The vacancy to unemployed ratio is 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8. Now demand strengthens and openings rise to 6 million while unemployment falls to 4 million. The unemployment rate is 4 percent and the ratio is 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5. That pair of moves (higher vacancies, lower unemployment) is a slide along a stable Beveridge curve. Separately, imagine openings stay at 4 million but matching worsens so unemployment rises to 6 million. The unemployment rate is 6 percent at the same vacancy level: an outward shift. Try the Okun's law calculator if you want the arithmetic that links a GDP growth gap to a change in unemployment, and the Phillips curve calculator for the inflation side of slack. Neither calculator fetches live JOLTS data.

Common exam mistakes

  • Calling every rise in vacancies a shift. If unemployment falls at the same time, the usual reading is a move along the curve, not an outward shift.
  • Treating the Beveridge curve as a Phillips curve. Vacancies and unemployment are one relationship. Inflation and unemployment are another. Do not swap the vertical axes.
  • Mixing a vacancy level with a vacancy rate. JTSJOL is thousands of openings. JTSJOR is a rate. UNRATE is a rate. UNEMPLOY is a level. Keep units consistent when you form a ratio.
  • Reading staff research as a policy promise. The Figura and Waller FEDS Note is useful for the framework. It is not an FOMC statement about the path of rates or unemployment.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Beveridge curve?

The Beveridge curve is the downward sloping relationship between the job vacancy rate and the unemployment rate. When vacancies are high, unemployment tends to be low, and when vacancies are low, unemployment tends to be high. US readers usually plot the BLS JOLTS job openings rate against the BLS unemployment rate.

Why does the Beveridge curve slope down?

In a standard search and matching model, more vacancies relative to unemployed workers raise the job finding rate, so steady state unemployment falls. A July 2022 Federal Reserve FEDS Note by Andrew Figura and Chris Waller derives that link from flows into and out of unemployment and a matching function that ties hires to vacancies and unemployment.

What makes the Beveridge curve shift?

A shift means a different vacancy rate at the same unemployment rate, or the reverse. The same FEDS Note lists lower matching efficiency and more reallocation as forces that push the curve out, raising unemployment for a given vacancy rate. Higher matching efficiency or faster matching pulls it in.

What are the latest US job openings and unemployment figures for the curve?

For September 2026, FRED series JTSJOR shows a total nonfarm job openings rate of 4.5 percent and JTSJOL shows 7,523 thousand openings, both seasonally adjusted from the BLS JOLTS release updated 3 November 2026. For the same month, FRED series UNRATE shows unemployment at 4.2 percent and UNEMPLOY shows 7,109 thousand unemployed people. The October 2026 unemployment rate on UNRATE was 4.1 percent. All figures were retrieved 19 November 2026.

How is the Beveridge curve different from the Phillips curve?

The Beveridge curve pairs vacancies with unemployment. The Phillips curve pairs unemployment with inflation. Both use labour market slack, but they answer different questions. This site has a separate Phillips curve explainer and calculator.
