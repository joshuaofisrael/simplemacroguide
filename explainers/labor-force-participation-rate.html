Labor force participation rate: definition and formula

Explainer · Labor market · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

The labor force participation rate is the share of the adult population that is in the labor force, meaning either employed or unemployed and actively looking for work. The FRED notes for the official US series quote the Current Population Survey definition: the number of people in the labor force as a percentage of the civilian noninstitutional population. In short, it is the percentage of the population that is either working or actively looking for work.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

The formula

Participation rate = (employed + unemployed) ÷ civilian noninstitutional population aged 16+ × 100

The denominator leaves out people under 16, people in the armed forces and people in institutions such as prisons. Everyone else aged 16 and over falls into one of three groups: employed, unemployed (no job but actively looking), or not in the labor force (students, retirees, carers and others not looking).

A hypothetical worked example

A country has 200 million adults in the civilian noninstitutional population. 120 million are employed and 6 million are unemployed. The labor force is 126 million, so participation is 126 ÷ 200 × 100 = 63% and unemployment is 6 ÷ 126 × 100 ≈ 4.8%. Now 2 million of the unemployed give up looking. The labor force drops to 124 million. Participation falls to 62%, and the unemployment rate falls to 4 ÷ 124 × 100 ≈ 3.2%, even though nobody found a job. These numbers are invented for teaching.

The latest US figure

FRED series CIVPART, which is BLS series LNS11300000, reported 61.9% for October 2026, up from 61.8% in September and 61.4% in July 2026, seasonally adjusted, retrieved 12 November 2026. The series runs monthly from January 1948.

Why economists watch it

The unemployment rate alone can mislead, as the example shows. Participation tells you whether people are drifting out of the labor force altogether. FRED's notes add a warning: long-run changes in participation can reflect trends unrelated to the health of the economy, such as an ageing population retiring. That is why analysts also watch participation among people aged 25 to 54, who are less affected by school and retirement.

Common exam mistakes

  • Dividing by the labor force. Participation divides by the adult civilian population.
  • Calling discouraged workers unemployed. If they are not looking, they are out of the labor force.
  • Assuming a falling unemployment rate always means more jobs. It can also mean people stopped looking.

Frequently asked questions

What is the labor force participation rate?

It is the labor force as a percentage of the civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 and over. In plain terms, it is the share of adults who are either working or actively looking for work.

How is it different from the unemployment rate?

The unemployment rate divides the unemployed by the labor force. The participation rate divides the labor force by the whole adult civilian population. Someone who stops looking for work leaves both the unemployed count and the labor force.

What was the US labor force participation rate most recently?

FRED reports 61.9% for October 2026, seasonally adjusted, from the BLS Current Population Survey, retrieved 12 November 2026.

Why can participation fall when the economy is fine?

An ageing population means more people retire, which lowers participation even when jobs are easy to find. FRED's notes point to this kind of long-run demographic effect.
