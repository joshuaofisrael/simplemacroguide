The business cycle, explained

Explainer · long form · UK and US framing · Educational only · Updated 5 Nov 2026

The business cycle is the repeated rise and fall of overall economic activity around its long run trend. An economy moves through an expansion, reaches a peak, falls through a contraction (a recession if it is significant), hits a trough, and then expands again. The phases are regular in name only: no two cycles have the same length or depth. Educational only; not investment advice.

The four phases

  • Expansion. Output, jobs and incomes grow. Unemployment usually falls and firms invest more.
  • Peak. The high point of activity before a downturn. Output is often above potential, so a positive output gap and building price pressure are common.
  • Contraction. Activity falls. If the decline is deep, broad and lasting, it is called a recession.
  • Trough. The low point. Output is usually below potential, unemployment is high, and the next expansion starts from here.

Who decides when a cycle turns?

In the United States, the NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee sets the official peak and trough months. It looks at a range of monthly indicators, such as employment and real income, not only GDP, and it describes a recession as a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy that lasts more than a few months. Because it waits for enough data, its announcements usually come well after the turning point.

In the UK and many other countries there is no equivalent committee. Commentators commonly use a shorthand: two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. That rule is simple but can miss shallow or uneven downturns. Our recession definitions explainer compares the approaches in detail.

Why cycles happen

Economists point to shocks to demand (for example, a fall in consumer or business confidence, or a tightening of credit) and shocks to supply (for example, an energy price spike). In the AD/AS model, a leftward shift in aggregate demand lowers output and, in the short run, raises unemployment. Okun's law gives a rough link between output swings and unemployment changes.

What policy does across the cycle

Central banks usually cut interest rates in downturns and raise them when the economy runs hot, and automatic stabilisers such as tax receipts and unemployment benefits move against the cycle without new legislation. See monetary and fiscal policy and fiscal multipliers.

Common myths

  • Myth: cycles have a fixed length. Expansions and contractions vary widely in duration.
  • Myth: one weak GDP print means recession. Revisions are common, and breadth and duration matter.
  • Myth: the trend line is known. Potential output is estimated, so the size of any gap is uncertain.
