The liquidity trap and the zero lower bound explained

Explainer · Monetary and fiscal · Educational only · Updated 17 Nov 2026

A liquidity trap is a situation in which the central bank's usual tool, cutting the short term interest rate, stops working because that rate is already at or near zero. The limit itself is called the zero lower bound (or, since some central banks have set slightly negative rates, the effective lower bound). The US Federal Reserve held its target range at 0 to 0.25 percent from December 2008 to December 2015 and again from March 2020 to March 2022, so this is not just a textbook curiosity.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice.

What a liquidity trap is

In his 1998 Brookings paper on Japan, "It's Baaack: Japan's Slump and the Return of the Liquidity Trap", Paul Krugman describes the liquidity trap as the awkward condition in which monetary policy loses its grip because the nominal interest rate is essentially zero, and the quantity of money becomes irrelevant because money and bonds are essentially perfect substitutes. The idea goes back to John Hicks, who introduced both the IS LM model and the liquidity trap.

The intuition is simple. Cash pays a nominal interest rate of zero. Once short term bonds also pay about zero, people and banks have no reason to prefer the bond, so a central bank that swaps new money for bonds just leaves them holding more money that they do not spend. Krugman notes that at the end of the 1930s US rates were hard up against that constraint: the average rate on US Treasury bills during 1940 was 0.014 percent.

Why there is a lower bound at all

Ordinary rate cuts work by making borrowing cheaper and saving less rewarding, which raises spending (see the money market and loanable funds explainer). If a central bank tried to push rates far below zero, savers could hold paper cash instead, which always earns zero. That option is what puts a floor under rates. The floor is not exactly zero, because holding and storing large amounts of cash is costly, so a central bank can go a little below zero before people switch.

The euro area shows this. The European Central Bank's table of key interest rates shows its deposit facility rate moved to minus 0.10 percent from 11 June 2014, was cut in steps to minus 0.50 percent from 18 September 2019, and returned to 0.00 percent from 27 July 2022. Rates below zero were possible, but only by a small margin, which is why economists now often say "effective lower bound" rather than "zero lower bound".

Why it matters: the real interest rate

What matters for spending is the real interest rate, roughly the nominal rate minus expected inflation. At the lower bound the nominal rate cannot fall further, so if expected inflation drops, the real rate rises even though the central bank has done nothing. That is the danger in a slump with falling prices: weaker demand lowers inflation, which raises real rates, which weakens demand again.

A hypothetical worked example

The numbers below are made up for teaching. Take the classic Taylor rule sketch used on this site: suggested policy rate = equilibrium real rate + inflation + 0.5 × (inflation minus target) + 0.5 × (output gap).

  • Equilibrium real rate 0.5 percent, inflation 0.5 percent, target 2 percent, output gap minus 6 percent.
  • Suggested rate = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 × (0.5 minus 2) + 0.5 × (minus 6) = 1 minus 0.75 minus 3 = minus 2.75 percent.

The rule asks for a deeply negative rate that the central bank cannot deliver. The gap between the rate it would like and the lowest rate it can set is the shortfall the other tools below try to fill. What policymakers do at the lower bound

Large scale asset purchases. The Federal Reserve's open market operations page notes that from the end of 2008 through October 2014 the Fed greatly expanded its holdings of longer term securities through open market purchases, with the goal of putting downward pressure on longer term interest rates. This is quantitative easing.

Forward guidance. Promising to keep the policy rate low for longer can lower longer term rates today, because those rates reflect expected future short rates (see the yield curve explainer). The forward guidance explainer on this site covers the open ended, calendar and threshold based types central banks have actually used, with dated Fed, ECB and Bank of England examples.

How much do these tools help? In his 2020 American Economic Association lecture, "The New Tools of Monetary Policy", Ben Bernanke argues that QE and forward guidance have proven effective at easing financial conditions when policy rates are at the lower bound. He reports simulations of the Fed's FRB/US model suggesting that, if the nominal neutral interest rate is around 2 to 3 percent, the two tools together can provide the equivalent of roughly 3 percentage points of extra policy space. If the neutral rate is much lower, he says, overcoming the lower bound may need more, such as a moderate increase in the inflation target or greater reliance on fiscal policy.

Fiscal policy. Government spending and tax changes do not rely on the policy rate, so they become more important in a liquidity trap. The fiscal multipliers and Keynesian cross explainers show the spending side, and the paradox of thrift explains why everyone saving more at once can deepen a slump.

US episodes at the lower bound

The Federal Reserve's table of changes to the federal funds target shows two long spells at a target range of 0 to 0.25 percent:

  • 16 December 2008 to 17 December 2015: the range was cut to 0 to 0.25 percent during the financial crisis and first raised to 0.25 to 0.50 percent seven years later.
  • 16 March 2020 to 17 March 2022: the range was cut by a full percentage point to 0 to 0.25 percent at the start of the pandemic and raised to 0.25 to 0.50 percent two years later.

The federal funds rate explainer covers how the Fed sets and steers that rate.

Common exam mistakes

  • Saying monetary policy can always fix a recession. In a liquidity trap the usual rate cut is not available, so the answer usually turns to fiscal policy or unconventional tools.
  • Thinking the money supply cannot rise. It can. The point is that extra money is simply held rather than spent, so it does little for output.
  • Mixing up nominal and real rates. The bound applies to the nominal rate. The real rate can still rise if expected inflation falls.
  • Treating zero as an exact floor. The ECB set a negative deposit rate for eight years, so the bound is "effective", slightly below zero.

Frequently asked questions

What is a liquidity trap in simple terms?

It is when interest rates are already near zero, so the central bank cannot cut them further to boost spending, and adding more money does little because people and banks simply hold it. Paul Krugman describes it as the condition in which monetary policy loses its grip because the nominal interest rate is essentially zero.

What is the zero lower bound?

It is the floor on nominal interest rates that exists because cash always pays zero. If rates went far below zero, savers could switch to cash. In practice the floor is slightly below zero, which is why it is also called the effective lower bound.

Has the United States been in a liquidity trap?

The Federal Reserve held its federal funds target range at 0 to 0.25 percent from 16 December 2008 to 17 December 2015 and from 16 March 2020 to 17 March 2022. During those spells it could not cut its policy rate further and relied on asset purchases and guidance about future rates.

Can interest rates go below zero?

A little. The European Central Bank's deposit facility rate was negative from 11 June 2014 until 27 July 2022 and reached minus 0.50 percent. Rates cannot go far below zero for long because people could hold cash instead.

How do you get out of a liquidity trap?

The usual answers are fiscal policy (government spending or tax cuts), quantitative easing, and forward guidance that promises low rates for longer. Ben Bernanke's 2020 lecture argues that QE and forward guidance together can provide roughly 3 percentage points of extra policy space if the neutral rate is around 2 to 3 percent.
