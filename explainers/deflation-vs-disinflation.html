Deflation vs disinflation: what is the difference?

Explainer · Inflation · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

Deflation is a decline in the general price level: the inflation rate is negative, so a typical basket of goods costs less than it did a year earlier. Disinflation is a slowdown in inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly than before. If inflation falls from 5% to 2%, that is disinflation. If it falls from 1% to minus 1%, the economy has moved into deflation.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

The definitions

The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis puts it simply in a 2023 Open Vault post: inflation is a sustained increase in the price level, disinflation is a decrease in the rate of inflation, and deflation is a sustained decrease in the price level. The same post gives a historical case. In 2008, US CPI inflation slowed from July to November, which was disinflation, and from March to October 2009 the CPI was lower than a year earlier, which was deflation. Its Page One Economics lesson makes the same point for students: with disinflation, prices are still going up, just not as fast.

The key is to keep two things apart. The price level is how much things cost, measured by an index such as the CPI or the PCE price index. The inflation rate is the percentage change in that index. Disinflation is about the rate falling. Deflation is about the level falling.

A hypothetical worked example

Suppose a price index is 100 in year 1. It rises to 106 in year 2, which is 6% inflation. It rises to 108.12 in year 3, which is 2% inflation. Between year 2 and year 3 the economy had disinflation: inflation fell from 6% to 2%, yet the index still went up. If the index then dropped to 107 in year 4, inflation would be about minus 1%, and that would be deflation. These numbers are invented for teaching.

A dated US example of disinflation

US Bureau of Economic Analysis data show the PCE price index rose at about a 5.0% annual rate in the second quarter of 2026 and about a 2.1% annual rate in the third quarter, according to the Q3 2026 advance estimate released 29 October 2026. You can check the index on FRED series PCECTPI. That is disinflation: the inflation rate fell sharply, but it stayed positive, so prices kept rising. It was not deflation.

Quarterly annualized rates can swing a lot, so economists also look at 12 month changes and at core measures that leave out food and energy. The core vs headline inflation explainer and the CPI vs PCE explainer cover those choices.

Why deflation worries policymakers

The St. Louis Fed notes that many economists consider deflation harmful, although that claim is disputed. The usual arguments are these:

  • Debt gets heavier. Loans are fixed in money terms. When prices and wages fall, the real burden of repaying them rises.
  • Spending can be delayed. If people expect prices to keep falling, waiting to buy can look sensible, which weakens demand further.
  • Interest rates hit a floor. Nominal rates cannot easily go far below zero. With falling prices, the real interest rate can stay high even when the nominal rate is near zero. See the real interest rates explainer.

Disinflation, by contrast, is often the goal, though the process can be painful. The St. Louis Fed post points to the early 1980s, when the Federal Reserve brought CPI inflation down from 14.6% in March 1980 to 2.4% in July 1983 while the unemployment rate rose from 6.3% to a peak of 10.8% in November 1982. When inflation is well above a central bank's target, policy aims to bring it down without pushing it below zero.

Where each shows up in AP Macro

On the aggregate demand and aggregate supply graph, a slower rightward drift of aggregate demand, or a positive supply shock, slows the rise in the price level: disinflation. A large leftward shift of aggregate demand that actually lowers the price level is deflation. On the short run Phillips curve, disinflation is a move down to a lower inflation rate, often with higher unemployment in the short run.

Common exam mistakes

  • Saying prices fell when inflation dropped from 4% to 2%. They rose by 2%.
  • Calling any fall in inflation deflation. Deflation needs a negative rate.
  • Mixing up the price level and the inflation rate on graphs and in answers.
  • Assuming falling prices for one product, such as televisions, mean deflation. Deflation is about the general price level.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between deflation and disinflation?

Deflation is a fall in the general price level, so the inflation rate is below zero. Disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while it stays above zero, so prices keep rising but more slowly.

Is disinflation good or bad?

It depends on where inflation starts. Moving from high inflation back toward a central bank's target is usually the aim of policy. Disinflation that overshoots into deflation can be harmful.

Why do central banks worry about deflation?

Falling prices raise the real value of debts, and if people expect prices to keep falling they may delay spending. Interest rates also cannot easily go far below zero, which limits how much a central bank can cut real rates.

Do prices go down during disinflation?

No. During disinflation the price level still rises. Only the speed of the rise slows.
