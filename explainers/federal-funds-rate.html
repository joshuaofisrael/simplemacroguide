Federal funds rate: what it is and how to read it

Explainer · Monetary policy · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

The federal funds rate is the interest rate that depository institutions charge each other for overnight loans of reserve balances. When one bank has extra reserves at the Fed and another is short, they lend overnight in the federal funds market. The rate on those loans is the federal funds rate. It is the main short-term policy rate in US monetary policy.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

Effective rate versus target range

Two closely related numbers appear in the data. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meets eight times a year and sets a target range for the federal funds rate, according to FRED's notes on the series. The effective federal funds rate is the rate that actually prevails in the overnight market. The Fed then uses tools, including the interest on reserve balances rate, to steer the effective rate toward that range. This page reports the effective rate from FRED. It does not print a target range that those series pages do not print.

How to read the FRED series

FRED publishes the effective rate in two standard forms:

  • FEDFUNDS is a monthly average, in percent, not seasonally adjusted, from the Board of Governors (H.15).
  • EFFR is a daily series, also in percent and not seasonally adjusted, associated with the New York Fed.

A monthly average can hide a mid-month policy change. If the FOMC moves the target in the last week of a month, the daily series will show the new level sooner than the monthly average will.

A hypothetical worked example

Bank A has $10 million of idle reserves and Bank B is $10 million short of the reserves it wants to hold overnight. Bank A lends Bank B $10 million at 4.00% for one day. The interest is 4.00% × (1/360) × $10 million under the usual money-market day count, or about $1,111. These numbers are invented for teaching. In the real market the effective rate is a volume-weighted average of many such trades, not a single loan.

The latest figures

FRED reports a monthly effective rate of 3.88% for October 2026, up from 3.75% in September and 3.63% from May through August 2026, retrieved 12 November 2026. The daily series EFFR was 3.88% on 10 November 2026, and it held at 3.88% on each of the preceding business days shown in the download through 30 October, also retrieved 12 November 2026.

Why it shows up on the exam

In the money market, a higher policy rate makes holding money more costly and tends to cool borrowing and spending. A lower rate works in the other direction. The Taylor rule is one textbook formula for how a central bank might set that rate in response to inflation and the output gap. In the current ample-reserves regime, the Fed steers the funds rate with administered rates rather than by making reserves scarce, as the reserve market explainer describes.

Common exam mistakes

  • Calling the federal funds rate the rate the Fed charges banks at the discount window. That is the discount rate, a different administered rate.
  • Treating the target range as the same thing as the effective rate. The target is a policy setting; the effective rate is a market outcome.
  • Reading FEDFUNDS as a daily observation. It is a monthly average.

Frequently asked questions

What is the federal funds rate?

It is the interest rate depository institutions charge each other for overnight loans of reserve balances. FRED describes both FEDFUNDS and EFFR this way.

What is the difference between the effective rate and the target range?

The Federal Open Market Committee sets a target range. The effective federal funds rate is the rate that actually trades in the overnight market. FRED's notes say the Fed uses the interest on reserve balances rate to steer the effective rate toward that range.

What is the latest effective federal funds rate?

FRED's monthly series FEDFUNDS was 3.88% in October 2026. The daily series EFFR was 3.88% on 10 November 2026. Both were retrieved 12 November 2026.

Why does the federal funds rate matter in AP Macro?

It is the policy rate at the center of the money market. Other short-term rates tend to move with it, which is how monetary policy works through to borrowing, spending and, with a lag, inflation and output.
