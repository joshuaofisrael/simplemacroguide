The Keynesian cross explained: the income expenditure model

Explainer · AP Macroeconomics · Educational only · Updated 11 Nov 2026

The Keynesian cross, also called the expenditure output or income expenditure model, finds equilibrium real GDP at the point where total planned spending in the economy equals total output. On the diagram, that is where the aggregate expenditure line crosses a 45 degree line. If spending falls short of output, unsold goods pile up and firms cut production. If spending runs ahead, shelves empty and firms produce more. The same diagram shows the spending multiplier: a shift in spending moves equilibrium output by more than the shift itself.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

The axes and the 45 degree line

The OpenStax textbook Principles of Macroeconomics puts real GDP on the horizontal axis as a measure of output and aggregate expenditure on the vertical axis as a measure of spending, both in real terms. Because every sale of final output ends up as income for someone, the horizontal axis can also be read as national income, written Y.

The 45 degree line starts at the origin and joins every point where the two axes are equal: spending of 1,000 with output of 1,000, spending of 2,000 with output of 2,000, and so on. Any equilibrium has to sit on it, because in equilibrium what is spent equals what is produced. OpenStax also draws a vertical line at potential GDP, the output the economy can produce with full employment of its labour and capital, as a second guidepost.

Building the aggregate expenditure line

Aggregate expenditure (AE) is consumption plus investment plus government spending plus exports minus imports: AE = C + I + G + X minus M. The model asks how each part changes as national income rises.

  • Consumption rises with income. The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is the share of an extra dollar or pound of income that is spent, and the marginal propensity to save (MPS) is the share saved, so MPC + MPS = 1. OpenStax's example assumes 600 is consumed even at zero income and an MPC of 0.8, so at an income of 4,000 consumption is 600 + 0.8 × 4,000 = 3,800.
  • Investment is drawn flat. It depends mainly on expected returns, interest rates and expectations about the future rather than on this year's GDP. It can still shift up or down when those drivers change.
  • Government spending is drawn flat because it is set by budget decisions, not by the current level of GDP.
  • Taxes are usually treated as a share of income. They reduce after tax income, so the consumption line becomes flatter.
  • Exports are flat because they depend on demand in other countries. Imports rise with domestic income at the marginal propensity to import (MPI) and are subtracted from spending.

Stack these and you get an upward sloping AE line that is flatter than the 45 degree line. Its intercept comes from the parts that do not depend on income. Its slope is the share of each extra unit of income that is spent on domestic output, which shrinks as saving, the tax rate or the MPI rise.

Why the crossing point is the equilibrium

OpenStax explains the adjustment with two points off the crossing. At an output above equilibrium, the AE line is below the 45 degree line, so spending is less than output and goods pile up unsold. Firms respond by cutting production. At an output below equilibrium, AE is above the 45 degree line, so spending exceeds output and shelves are emptied. Firms respond by producing more. Only at the crossing do production and spending balance, so there is no pressure to change.

In exam answers this is often described through inventories: unplanned inventory building signals output is too high, and unplanned inventory drawdown signals output is too low.

A worked example with made up numbers

Take a simple closed economy with no taxes on income. Consumption is C = 100 + 0.75Y, investment is 150 and government spending is 250. Then:

  • Aggregate expenditure is AE = 100 + 0.75Y + 150 + 250 = 500 + 0.75Y.
  • Set AE equal to Y: Y = 500 + 0.75Y, so 0.25Y = 500 and Y = 2,000.
  • Check: consumption at Y = 2,000 is 100 + 1,500 = 1,600. Add 150 and 250 and spending is 2,000, which equals output.
  • At Y = 2,400, spending is 500 + 1,800 = 2,300, so 100 of output goes unsold and firms cut back. At Y = 1,600, spending is 500 + 1,200 = 1,700, which is 100 more than output, so firms expand.

The multiplier on the diagram

Now raise government spending by 50, from 250 to 300. The AE line shifts up by 50 to 550 + 0.75Y. Solving again, 0.25Y = 550, so Y = 2,200. Output rose by 200, four times the change in spending.

That ratio is the spending multiplier. In this simple model it is 1 divided by (1 minus MPC), which here is 1 divided by 0.25, or 4. The reason is the chain of spending OpenStax describes: the extra 50 is income for someone, who spends 75 percent of it, which is income for someone else, and so on, with each round smaller than the last. The vertical shift of the AE line is small, but the horizontal move in equilibrium output is larger.

Taxes and imports are leakages that make each round smaller. OpenStax gives the general formula as 1 divided by (1 minus (MPC × (1 minus tax rate) minus MPI)). In its worked example, people pay 30 percent of income in tax, save 10 percent of after tax income and spend 10 percent of income on imports, so 53 cents of each extra dollar is spent on domestic output in the next round. The multiplier is 1 divided by 0.47, about 2.13, so an extra 100 of government spending raises equilibrium GDP by about 213. You can run the simple and open economy versions in the fiscal multiplier calculator.

Recessionary and inflationary gaps

Nothing in the model guarantees that equilibrium lands at potential GDP. If the AE line crosses the 45 degree line below potential, there is a recessionary gap: spending is too low to employ everyone, and OpenStax notes the economy can stay stuck there because equilibrium means there is no automatic pressure to change. Anything that shifts AE down, such as a fall in consumption, investment, government spending or exports, or a rise in taxes or imports, can open one.

If the crossing is above potential, there is an inflationary gap. OpenStax warns against reading this literally as output far beyond what the economy can make. The economy faces supply limits, so the extra spending shows up as a rising price level instead. That is why the model is usually paired with the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model, which shows prices explicitly. The gap here is the same idea as the output gap measured against potential.

Because of the multiplier, closing a gap needs a smaller change in spending than the gap itself. In OpenStax's example, equilibrium is 700 and potential is 800, and with a multiplier of 2.13 an increase in government spending of about 47 closes the 100 gap.

What the simple model leaves out

The Keynesian cross holds the price level and interest rates fixed. In real economies, a fiscal expansion can raise interest rates and reduce private investment (see crowding out), the central bank may respond, and prices adjust. That is why measured multipliers are conditional estimates that vary with slack and policy, as the fiscal multipliers explainer discusses. OpenStax also points to a trade off: a high multiplier makes policy more powerful but also makes the economy less stable, because every shock to spending is magnified. Built in leakages such as income taxes are part of why automatic stabilizers damp the cycle.

Common exam mistakes

  • Drawing the AE line steeper than the 45 degree line. With an MPC below 1 and positive leakages, the AE line is flatter, which is what makes the crossing stable.
  • Saying firms raise output when inventories pile up. Unplanned inventory building means output is above equilibrium, so firms cut production.
  • Closing a recessionary gap with a spending change equal to the gap. Divide the gap by the multiplier instead.
  • Using 1 divided by (1 minus MPC) once taxes and imports are in the question. Leakages make the multiplier smaller.
  • Reading an inflationary gap as real output above capacity. In this model it signals upward pressure on prices.
  • Mixing up a movement along the AE line, caused by a change in income, with a shift of the line, caused by a change in investment, government spending, taxes, exports or autonomous consumption.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Keynesian cross in simple terms?

It is a diagram that finds the level of real GDP at which total planned spending equals total output. Aggregate expenditure is on the vertical axis, real GDP on the horizontal axis, and equilibrium is where the spending line crosses the 45 degree line.

Why is it called a cross?

Because the answer is read off where two lines cross: the upward sloping aggregate expenditure line and the 45 degree line that marks every point where spending equals output.

How does the Keynesian cross show the multiplier?

When spending shifts the aggregate expenditure line up, equilibrium output rises by more than the vertical shift. In the simplest model the ratio is 1 divided by (1 minus the marginal propensity to consume), so an MPC of 0.75 gives a multiplier of 4. Taxes and imports make it smaller.

What is the difference between the Keynesian cross and the AD AS model?

The Keynesian cross holds the price level fixed and focuses on spending, so it shows how demand sets output in the short run. The aggregate demand and aggregate supply model adds the price level and supply limits, so it can show inflation and the return toward potential GDP.
