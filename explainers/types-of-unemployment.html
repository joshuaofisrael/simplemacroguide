Types of unemployment: frictional, structural and cyclical

Explainer · Labor · Educational only · Updated 12 Nov 2026

Economists split unemployment into three types because they have different causes and different remedies. Frictional and structural unemployment exist even when the economy is healthy. Cyclical unemployment appears when demand falls in a recession. OpenStax Principles of Macroeconomics 3e uses this split in Chapter 8 to explain why the unemployment rate never hits zero and why it jumps in downturns.

This page is for AP Macroeconomics and intro economics study. It is educational only and is not investment, financial, tax, or legal advice. It makes no forecast about any economy.

Frictional unemployment

Frictional unemployment is the time it takes people to move between jobs in a changing economy. Firms close and other firms hire. Even when the number of job seekers equals the number of vacancies, it still takes time to hear about an opening, interview and decide whether the match is good, or move. OpenStax stresses that this is not inherently a bad thing: you want people in jobs that fit, not just the first opening they see.

Structural unemployment

Structural unemployment is a longer mismatch. OpenStax's wording is that people have no jobs because they lack skills the labor market values, either because demand has shifted away from the skills they have or because they never learned those skills. A classic textbook case is a specialist whose industry shrinks permanently. Retraining and education can shrink this type of unemployment; a short spell of job search will not.

Cyclical unemployment

Cyclical unemployment rises and falls with the business cycle. In a recession, firms need fewer workers. If wages do not fall quickly enough to clear the labor market, OpenStax notes, the result is unemployment above the natural rate. When the economy expands, this component shrinks and can even go negative for a time, meaning the official rate dips below the natural rate.

How the types add up

Unemployment rate ≈ frictional + structural + cyclical

The natural rate of unemployment is frictional plus structural. Full employment, in this vocabulary, does not mean zero unemployment: it means the official rate is at the natural rate and cyclical unemployment is about zero. OpenStax also ties that point to potential GDP: at full employment, real GDP is at potential.

A hypothetical worked example

Suppose a labor force of 100 million people includes 2 million frictionally unemployed, 2 million structurally unemployed and 1 million cyclically unemployed. The unemployment rate is 5%. The natural rate is 4%. If the cyclical group finds work in an expansion, the rate falls to 4% even though frictional and structural unemployment remain. These numbers are invented for teaching.

The latest US figure

FRED series UNRATE, which is the official U-3 unemployment rate from the Current Population Survey, reported 4.1% in October 2026, down from 4.2% in September and 4.3% in May 2026, seasonally adjusted, retrieved 12 November 2026. FRED's notes define this as the number of people aged 16 and over who are actively looking for work, as a percentage of the labor force. The series does not split that 4.1% into frictional, structural and cyclical parts; those labels are an analytical breakdown, not three official BLS headline rates. For a broader official measure, see U-3 vs U-6.

Common exam mistakes

  • Treating a person who just quit to look for a better job as cyclically unemployed. That is frictional.
  • Calling a factory closure in a shrinking industry cyclical when the jobs are not coming back. That is structural.
  • Saying full employment means zero unemployment. It means no cyclical unemployment.
  • Dividing the unemployed by the adult population. The official rate divides by the labor force. See the participation rate explainer.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three types of unemployment?

AP Macro groups unemployment into frictional (time spent moving between jobs), structural (a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and the jobs on offer), and cyclical (unemployment that rises in a recession and falls in an expansion).

What is the natural rate of unemployment?

OpenStax defines it as the unemployment that remains even when the economy is neither in a boom nor a recession. It is frictional plus structural unemployment, not cyclical unemployment.

Does the unemployment rate ever fall to zero?

No. OpenStax notes that some frictional and structural unemployment remains even in good times, so the official rate almost never gets below about 3%, and then only briefly.
